Inspection vs. grading

Inspection is mandatory. A USDA inspector confirms the meat is wholesome (fit for people to eat) and labeled accurately. It says nothing about quality.

Grading is voluntary and paid for by the packer. It comes in two kinds: quality grades predict eating quality (tenderness, juiciness, flavor), and yield grades (1–5) predict how much usable lean meat the carcass gives. Yield grade 1 has the most usable meat.

USDA quality grades, highest to lowest

GradeWhere you’ll see it
PrimeFine-dining steakhouses; most marbling

Standard / CommercialOften sold ungraded or under store brands

What the grader looks at

  • Marbling – flecks of fat inside the lean. The single biggest factor in quality grade.
  • Maturity – age of the animal, judged by bone and cartilage. Younger beef is more tender.
  • Lean color, texture and firmness – bright cherry-red, fine-grained and firm is best.

Packers can also use private brand programs such as Certified Angus Beef, which add their own standards on top of USDA grades.

Aging beef

After slaughter, rigor mortis stiffens the muscle. Natural enzymes then slowly break down the proteins, making the meat more tender and flavorful. Properly aged beef never smells or tastes spoiled. If it does, it is spoiled.

How beef is purchased

From largest to smallest: whole carcass → side (half, split down the backbone) → quarters (fore ~52%, hind ~48%) → primals → subprimals → fabricated cuts (portion-control steaks and roasts).

Purveyors use IMPS/NAMP numbers from the Meat Buyer’s Guide so a chef in Reading and a supplier in Chicago mean exactly the same cut. For example, 112A is a lip-on ribeye and 180 is a strip loin.

Key separations

  • Chuck from rib: between the 5th and 6th ribs.
  • Rib from short loin (fore from hind): between the 12th and 13th ribs. The rib primal keeps 7 ribs, 6 through 12.
Matching cut to cooking method

Muscles that work hard (shoulder, legs, chest) have more connective tissue and need moist heat or combination methods to turn collagen into gelatin. Muscles along the middle of the back barely work, so they’re tender enough for dry heat.

Doneness and food safety

A thermometer is the only reliable test. Pull large roasts 5–10°F early because carryover cooking keeps raising the temperature while they rest.

DonenessFinal internal tempLook
Rare125°FBright red center, cool
Medium-rare130–135°FRed-pink center, warm
Medium140–145°FPink center
Medium-well150–155°FSlight pink
Well done160°F and upGray-brown throughout

ServSafe minimums (FDA Food Code)

  • Steaks and chops: 145°F for 15 seconds.
  • Whole beef roasts: 145°F for 4 minutes.
  • Ground beef and mechanically tenderized beef: 155°F for 17 seconds.

Guests may order whole-muscle steaks undercooked, but the menu must carry a consumer advisory. Rarer doneness is safe on intact steaks because bacteria live on the surface; grinding or needling pushes them inside.

Tenderizing and variety meats

Tenderizing breaks down muscle fibers or connective tissue either mechanically (mallet, cubing machine, needle tenderizer) or chemically (acidic marinades, enzyme powders like papain from papaya).

Variety meats (offal) include liver, heart, kidneys, tongue, tripe, oxtail, and sweetbreads (thymus gland, usually from veal). Tongue and heart need long simmering; liver and sweetbreads cook quickly.

Receiving and storage
  • Receive fresh beef at 41°F or lower; frozen beef should arrive frozen solid with no ice crystals or fluid stains (signs of thaw and refreeze).
  • Store raw beef covered, labeled and dated, on shelves below ready-to-eat food. Ground beef goes below whole cuts.
  • Hold fresh meat in a 34–38°F walk-in for best shelf life.
  • Thaw in the refrigerator, under cold running water (70°F or lower), in the microwave if cooking right away, or as part of cooking. Never on the counter.
  • Cooking from frozen takes roughly 1.5 times as long.
